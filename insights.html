What we are seeing in the market — salaries and packages, nationalisation, ramp-up rosters and the disciplines that are about to get harder to hire. Written by the consultants doing the searches, not by a content team.

What Saudi mining operations are actually paying technical staff

The single most common question on a first call, from both sides of the table, is what a role is worth. The honest answer in Saudi mining is that base salary is the least interesting part of the package, and comparing base alone is how both employers and candidates end up disappointed.

What actually moves the number

  • Residential versus rotational. The same role can differ by a wide margin depending on whether it is family-status residential, single-status residential or a FIFO rotation — and the two are not comparable on base alone.
  • Housing and schooling. On family-status packages these frequently matter more than a base increase, and they are where offers are won and lost.
  • Commodity and method. Underground hard-rock experience commands a premium over surface work at equivalent seniority, because the regional pool is far thinner.
  • Statutory competencies. A Registered Mine Manager ticket, or Competent Person status under JORC or NI 43-101, is a step change rather than an increment.
  • Stage of operation. Commissioning and ramp-up roles carry a premium over steady-state equivalents, and are usually the shortest to fill.

Where the gaps are widest

The largest expectation gaps we see are in processing and metallurgy, where international candidates benchmark against Australian or Canadian packages, and in mid-level technical services, where the regional market has moved faster than most internal salary bands have.

Saudization in mining: meeting the target without losing the plant

Nationalisation is not a compliance checkbox in mining, it is a workforce design problem. The operators getting it right are the ones who stopped treating it as a hiring quota and started treating it as a capability transfer programme with a schedule attached.

The failure mode

The pattern we see repeatedly: an operation hires to the percentage, places national staff into roles without a structured development path, and finds two years later that the target is met on paper while the technical capability still sits entirely with a handful of expatriate specialists who are now the only people who can run the plant. That is a target met and a risk created.

What works instead

  • Name the successor in the mandate. Every senior expatriate hire is recruited against a named or to-be-named successor and a handover horizon, written into the brief rather than bolted on afterwards.
  • Build the pipeline three years ahead. Graduate, apprentice and technical-institute intakes aimed at the disciplines the site will be short of in three years, not the ones it is short of today.
  • Go after returning talent. Saudi professionals with international mining experience are the fastest route to genuine national technical leadership, and they are the most contested people in the market.
  • Make the competency framework real. Assessed, signed-off progression rather than time served — the same discipline you would apply to a statutory ticket.

Why ramp-up rosters slip, and what to do about it 12 months earlier

Projects rarely miss nameplate because the plant will not run. They miss it because the people who know how to run the plant are not there yet. Almost every hiring decision that determines whether an operation reaches steady state on schedule is made before first blast.

The three compressions

  • Construction and operations overlap. Commissioning specialists and the first operating crews have to be on site while construction is still finishing, which means hiring against a schedule that has not stabilised.
  • The senior technical lead time. A General Manager or Head of Technical Services is realistically a four-to-six-month search from first conversation to site. If that search starts when the plant is nearly ready, it is already late.
  • Mobilisation is not instant. Medicals, police clearances, visa and iqama processing, and travel add weeks per person — and they add them at the exact point the schedule has no slack left.

What to do about it

Build the manning schedule as a hiring schedule, backwards from first ore, with the mobilisation lead time for each cohort built in rather than assumed away. Start the senior technical searches a full year before the seats are needed, and accept the carrying cost — it is a fraction of the cost of a delayed ramp-up. Then run the volume cohorts as campaigns against that plan rather than as a queue of requisitions.
